The Graham Instrument · The Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia Act of 2026 — the bill named for the Senator who spent three years drafting it and died in July before it reached a floor — cleared its final conference vote late Wednesday 16 September and was engrossed on the Speaker’s desk Thursday morning 17 September. The White House press office told the pool at 5:12 pm Eastern that the President will sign, and the West Wing signing calendar has held a Friday 18 September mid-afternoon slot open under the working title “Russia Sanctions.” The instrument itself hands the President three discretionary levers: secondary tariffs of up to one hundred per cent on exports from the top five purchasers of Russian energy, discretionary tariffs of up to five hundred per cent on Russian goods directly entering US customs territory, and an OFAC-adjacent facilitation-of-evasion authority against banks, insurers and shipping companies with a Russian nexus. Through the year to end-August, the top five buyers of Russian crude were China, India, Turkey, the United Arab Emirates and Serbia; the top five buyers of Russian pipeline gas and LNG were Hungary, Slovakia, Turkey, Serbia and the European Union under its residual pipeline flows. The specifically-material inversion the bill enacts is that the tariff instrument is no longer a bilateral trade grievance addressed at the counterparty; it is a coercive claim against every counterparty who transacts with the counterparty. Trump will not name a target list at Friday’s ceremony, and the West Wing has said nothing to Xi Jinping’s advance team ahead of the 24 September state visit that will collide with any first-week designation. Read as ordinary sanctions upgrade the Instrument absorbs. Read as statecraft it is the specifically-material moment at which the American executive publicly acquires a Damocles sword against the Chinese, Indian and Turkish energy-import trade, and reserves to itself the discretion to swing it, hold it, or trade it for concessions elsewhere — a discretion whose option value is higher than any single deployment. The Graham Instrument

Congress delivered the Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia Act of 2026 to the Speaker’s desk on Thursday 17 September after Wednesday’s conference vote closed the House-Senate reconciliation. The White House told the press pool the President will sign; the ceremony has held a Friday afternoon slot. The bill gives the executive discretionary authority to levy tariffs of up to one hundred per cent on exports from the top five purchasers of Russian energy, up to five hundred per cent on Russian goods entering US customs territory, and to designate banks, insurers and shipping companies facilitating evasion. The top five buyers of Russian crude through end-August were China, India, Turkey, the United Arab Emirates and Serbia; on pipeline gas and LNG the top five were Hungary, Slovakia, Turkey, Serbia and the European Union’s residual flows. The instrument enacts a specifically-material inversion: a bilateral sanctions grievance against Moscow is converted into a coercive claim against every counterparty who transacts with Moscow. The West Wing has said nothing to Xi Jinping’s advance team ahead of the 24 September state visit that will collide with any first-week Chinese designation. Read as ordinary sanctions upgrade the Instrument absorbs. Read as statecraft it is the specifically-material moment at which the American executive acquires a Damocles sword against the Chinese, Indian and Turkish energy-import trade — a discretion whose option value is higher un-pulled than pulled, and whose signing ceremony is therefore the beginning of a coercive-diplomacy calendar rather than the end of a sanctions-drafting one.

Mearsheimer on the hegemon that converts a bilateral quarrel into a system-wide instrument.

The offensive-realist discipline reads secondary sanctions as a specifically-material feature of hegemonic behaviour under conditions of contested unipolarity: unable to defeat the counterparty at the site of the quarrel (Ukraine, from which the Trump administration cannot extract the settlement it staked its first term on), the leading power reaches into the trading system that finances the counterparty and puts a price on continuing the transaction. What Mearsheimer’s frame names is not the moral valence of that move but its structural consequence: every state that buys Russian crude is now told, publicly, that the standing arrangement is transactional and revocable. China, India and Turkey are being asked to price the risk not of being sanctioned but of being sanctioned at the President’s discretion, which is a different pricing problem. The specifically-material Mearsheimer read is that this is how the American system consumes its own alliances: by naming a coalition it wishes to break up (Russia’s energy customers) it names, second-order, the coalition it depends on for its own trade equilibrium (the same states, sitting on the same tariff schedules). The tell inside three weeks is whether the Executive Order implementing the Act names anyone at all, or whether the West Wing keeps the tariff schedule empty and threatens rolling designations at each subsequent negotiation. The empty-schedule outcome is the higher-leverage one and, on the Mearsheimer read, the one Washington will prefer.

Sun Tzu Chapter III on attacking the counterparty’s coalition rather than its army.

Chapter III of the Art of War ranks the attack on the enemy’s alliances above the attack on the enemy’s army, because the alliance cost is compounding and the battlefield cost is spent. The Trump administration has already tried the army: the ATACMS releases of 2025, the December-2025 escalation-mediation offer, the February bilateral summit at Anchorage that produced no exit ramp. What the Graham Instrument now authorises is Chapter III at industrial scale: strike Russia’s revenue by pricing risk into every buyer of its crude, so that the counterparty’s war-financing coalition — not its front line — carries the cost. The specifically-material Sun Tzu tell is that the target is not the shell fired next week; it is the term of the Chinese refiner’s September crude contract, the pricing of the Reliance-and-BPCL Indian offtake calendar into October, and the Ceyhan-terminal insurance-and-reflagging cost that Turkish traders quote through Q4. If Trump extracts a Xi concession on the Ukraine file at the 24 September summit — a real one, not a communiqué — the Instrument will have paid for itself unused. If he does not, the first designation is priced by every marginal Russian barrel discounted five to seven dollars against Brent inside the fortnight. Either way the payload is the coalition, not the army.

Greene’s Law 3 on the instrument more useful un-pulled than pulled.

Robert Greene’s Third Law — Conceal Your Intentions — converts, at the register of sanctions policy, into a discipline of ambiguity: the operator whose credible threat covers ten targets and whose deployment names one has converted an option worth ten into an instrument worth one. The bill hands Trump exactly this option, and its highest-leverage use is therefore no use at all. Every sanctions administrator inside every mid-sized Russian oil buyer — the compliance desk at HDFC in Mumbai, the trade-finance floor at CIB in Shanghai, the Turkish state bank Ziraat — is now running an internal exposure audit that no OFAC designation has yet required. The specifically-material Law 3 payload is that the Instrument does its work through the audits it triggers rather than through the tariffs it imposes. Trump gains three separate negotiating leverages this week: over Xi on the 24 September summit, over Modi on the rare-earth-magnet pipeline discussed inside the Delhi Declaration a fortnight ago, and over Erdogan on the Turkstream continuation and the Ceyhan-terminal reflag. None of them will publicly concede; all of them will discreetly reprice. The concealment is the yield.

Voss on the ultimatum whose leverage collapses on delivery.

Chris Voss’s working discipline as an FBI hostage negotiator was that the ultimatum whose deadline is stated is one whose leverage compounds through the counterparty’s uncertainty; the ultimatum delivered on time expires. Trump’s sanctions cadence through the Ukraine war has been Vossian in exactly this respect: the 50-day ultimatum of July 2025 that was never enforced, the 10-day ultimatum of August 2025 that was never enforced, the November 2025 designation of Rosneft and Lukoil that was quietly softened by January licence-extensions. The Graham Instrument sits inside the same rhetorical repertoire but at a different register: it delegates the ultimatum instrument to the executive without requiring the executive to enforce it, which is the negotiator’s dream configuration. The specifically-material Voss tell is Trump’s Friday-afternoon language at the signing. If he names a country, the Instrument has been publicly booked and lost most of its leverage; if he names a class (“buyers of Russian oil,” “those who fund the war”), he preserves the ambiguity the tool depends on. The West Wing draft speaking-notes leaked to Reuters mid-Thursday used the class language, not the country language. That is the Voss read confirmed inside the West Wing itself.

What would collide the reading: a Friday-afternoon country designation (a public-commitment tell that would sacrifice option value for a headline); a first-week EO with a populated tariff schedule against China or India (a hard-instrument tell that would price the summit as a collision rather than a negotiation); a Chinese counter-move naming rare-earth-magnet export controls against US-facing supply chains inside the fortnight (a coalition-hardening tell that would score the Instrument as breaking its own frame); a Turkish or Indian public rejection of any secondary tariff (a firewall tell that would demonstrate the buyers can coordinate against a bilateral instrument); or a Ukrainian battlefield escalation absorbing the news cycle (a distraction tell that would delay the coercive-diplomacy calendar). Any of the five would move the reading from “Damocles sword acquired and un-swung” toward either “Instrument publicly booked and depreciating” or “coalition of buyers coordinated against the tool.”

Reading Note

The lenses used.

What’s driving this. Two layers carry the work. Structural: the specific mechanism is that a hegemonic power unable to compel the settlement at the site of the quarrel (Ukraine) reaches into the trading system that funds the counterparty and prices continued transaction with sanctions risk; the Instrument authorises the discretionary application of that pricing to five identifiable buyers, each with an internal compliance apparatus that begins running an exposure audit the moment the bill is signed. The audits do the work; the tariffs are the mechanism through which the audits become priced. Tactical: the specific mechanism is that the highest-leverage deployment of a discretionary sanctions authority is no deployment at all — the option value is maximised when the schedule is empty and the target list is a range rather than a country; the moment the President names a target he has publicly booked the tool and lost the negotiating leverage it carries with three other counterparties simultaneously. The leaked class-language speaking-notes are the operator confirming this discipline from inside the White House.

What’s not driving this. Not the Ukrainian battlefield. The Instrument reaches Trump’s desk after a summer in which the tactical situation in Donbas and around Zaporizhzhia has not materially moved and after Trump had already told Congress in June and again in August that he would not sign a stand-alone Ukraine aid bill. What changed is the leverage geometry: Xi’s state visit is a week away and Trump wants a real deliverable, so the bill’s arrival on his desk this week is a specifically-material timing choice by Congress and the White House together, not a response to a fresh Ukrainian move.

What would break this reading.

  • A Friday afternoon country designation at the signing ceremony — the West Wing would have publicly booked the Instrument and traded option value for a headline, and every subsequent negotiation would run against a range shrunk to whatever remains after the first name is out.
  • A first-week Executive Order with a populated tariff schedule against China or India — a hard-instrument tell that would price the 24 September Xi summit as a collision rather than a negotiation, and would force Beijing to publicly retaliate on rare-earth-magnet export controls before Trump has extracted any Ukraine-file concession.
  • A Chinese, Indian or Turkish coordinated counter-move naming a joint response to secondary tariffs inside the fortnight — a buyer-coalition tell that would demonstrate the targets can coordinate against a bilateral instrument and would move the reading from “Damocles sword” to “instrument the coalition of buyers has priced and neutralised.”
  • A Ukrainian battlefield escalation absorbing the UNGA news cycle — a distraction tell that would push the coercive-diplomacy calendar into November and let the audits inside the buyer-states relax before the Instrument is deployed once, at which point the schedule ambiguity is dissipated by time rather than by trade.

Graham Sanctioning Russia Act of 2026 to the Speaker’s desk Thursday morning and the White House pool briefing committed a Friday afternoon signing whose leaked speaking-notes use “buyers of Russian oil” class language rather than a country target list — every compliance desk from HDFC in Mumbai to CIB in Shanghai to Ziraat in Ankara is now running an internal exposure audit no Treasury designation has yet required, and the audit is the payload. From Dumfries House in Ayrshire, King Charles convened Sam Altman, Dario Amodei, Demis Hassabis and Jensen Huang under the King’s Trust, the King’s Foundation and the Sustainable Markets Initiative, opened with an “existential danger” frame the CEOs themselves have been publishing for four years, and received a draft principles statement authored inside the room by the operators who will be least burdened by its compliance overhead. From Schwerin and the Rotes Rathaus, Manuela Schwesig and Kai Wegner spent the closing forty-eight hours before Sunday’s twin ballots opening every coalition door they could keep open, so that the Sunday-evening arithmetic could decide which door is walked through rather than the pre-ballot signalling. Three instruments; three concealed intentions (a tariff range rather than a target, a norm rather than a rule, a coalition arithmetic rather than a coalition); three moves. The reader who reads the surface event is watching the signing, the summit, and the ballot. The reader who reads whether Trump names a country Friday afternoon, whether the FCDO communique carries the frontier firms’ own vocabulary, and whether Schwesig’s Sunday-evening exit interview names her coalition first as tradition or as arithmetic is watching the payload.”
The Stratagem · House Maxim
— Question for the Day —

Three operators today acquired or staged an instrument whose highest-leverage use is a use not yet made: Trump receives from Congress the discretionary secondary-tariff authority against China, India, Turkey, the UAE and Serbia and stages a Friday signing whose class-language speaking-notes preserve option value against all five at once; King Charles convenes four frontier AI CEOs at Dumfries House and lets them supply the vocabulary his sovereign-published framework will inherit, converting industry-drafted policy into a norm at a register that disables reputational audit; and Manuela Schwesig opens three coalition doors forty-eight hours before Sunday’s Mecklenburg-Vorpommern ballot she cannot win, so that Sunday-evening arithmetic rather than pre-ballot signalling names which door she walks through. Which discretionary tool have you just acquired that will lose leverage the moment you specify its target; which vocabulary has a sovereign-adjacent convenor invited you to supply, whose return in the sovereign voice will disable the audit rather than open it; and which forty-eight-hour window still lets you open multiple doors simultaneously — and which of them can you refuse to close until the counterparty’s count is public?
