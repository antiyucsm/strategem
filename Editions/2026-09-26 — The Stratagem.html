The Patriot Licence

On the sidelines of the UN General Assembly, President Donald Trump told President Volodymyr Zelenskyy that he had made a “final decision” to grant Ukraine a licence to manufacture the Patriot air-defence missile on its own territory. Zelenskyy announced it on Friday 25 September; the White House did not contradict him. Every prior US administration, including Trump’s first, had refused. The Patriot PAC-3 is the only Western interceptor that reliably kills the Iskander and Kinzhal ballistic missiles Russia now fires in the largest sustained tempo of the war, and Kyiv is burning through stock faster than Raytheon can build. A production licence solves the throughput problem in a way emergency shipments cannot — and it does something else Washington has spent three years avoiding. It embeds Ukraine inside the American missile-defence industrial base. That embedding is the story. It looks like a concession granted at the ally’s request; it is a concession the licence-giver will find, later, harder to reverse than the recipient.

Sun Tzu on the tool substituted for the fight.

Sun Tzu’s cleanest formulation, in Book III, is that the supreme excellence is not victory in a hundred battles but subduing the enemy without fighting. What underlies the aphorism is the discipline of substituting position for engagement — arranging conditions so that the outcome is decided before the shooting starts. Trump has spent nine months of his second term openly resisting exactly the escalatory step his predecessor never quite refused: putting American manufacturing inside a war zone bordering Russia. A Patriot production line in western Ukraine is that step, taken quietly, packaged as an ally’s concession. It commits Raytheon technicians, US Munitions List technology, and follow-on Pentagon spare-parts obligations to a facility Russia will consider a legitimate target. What the licence delivers is not additional missiles this quarter; it is a permanent Russian calculation that any strike on a Patriot line is a strike on American plant, that any Ukrainian negotiated peace will now include an American production interest to be preserved, and that any future US administration wishing to withdraw will find itself unwinding not aid packages but an industrial base. The tool has been substituted for the fight, and the tool is harder to remove than the aid was.

Greene’s Twenty-Second Law on the surrender that binds.

Use the Surrender Tactic — Transform Weakness into Power. Greene’s reading of the discipline is that when facing a stronger counterparty who is demanding something, granting it — wholly, formally, generously — can convert an obligation into a lever. Zelenskyy has pressed for licensed production since 2023. Every prior refusal preserved American discretion but at the cost of an accumulating diplomatic tab. Trump’s grant closes the tab in a way that changes the ledger. Kyiv now owes not a favour but a stake: American engineers on Ukrainian soil, American classified technology in a Ukrainian facility, an American revenue interest in what Ukraine builds. Every subsequent Ukrainian demand for aid, ceasefire posture, or negotiating flexibility is henceforth conditioned by the fact that Ukraine is a co-producer, not a client. Meanwhile, the concession splits the peace-deal architecture Trump wants: Putin has cooled on the technical channel Washington proposed in the UAE precisely because a licence for Ukrainian Patriot production reads, in the Kremlin, as the end of the negotiating window it hoped 2026 would open. The surrender tactic works when the counterparty finds the granted thing more binding than the withheld one would have been.

Mearsheimer on the structural insertion small states make into large industrial bases.

The structural realist reading of small-state security is that lasting protection comes not from written guarantees, which are trivially reversible, but from insertion into the physical architecture of the guarantor. Taiwan’s durability rests not on the Taiwan Relations Act but on TSMC. South Korea’s rests not on the Mutual Defence Treaty but on the Yongsan corridor of US bases and the KORUS-integrated production of everything from Hyundai chassis to Samsung memory in American supply chains. What Ukraine has extracted from the Trump concession is the same class of insertion, at the point of contact where the guarantor cares most: the missile that defends against the specific threat the guarantor’s own doctrine takes seriously. Once Patriot components are being machined in Lviv or Ivano-Frankivsk under licence from an American prime, Ukraine is not seeking protection from the US; it is holding a piece of the US that the US must protect to preserve its own reputation as a licensor. This is why Kyiv wanted the licence, not just the missiles. The missiles run out in a quarter. The insertion does not.

What the reading expects.

Base case through end-Q1 2027: a formal Memorandum of Understanding between the US Department of State (Directorate of Defense Trade Controls) and the Ukrainian Ministry of Strategic Industries publishes inside sixty days, naming Ukroboronprom or a Ukroboronprom-Raytheon joint entity as the licensed producer; groundbreaking on a hardened facility (most likely in the Lviv or Vinnytsia oblasts, at safe distance from the front) occurs by end-Q1 2027 but first delivery of Ukraine-produced PAC-3 rounds slips well into 2028; Russian rhetoric intensifies but no direct strike on the future site occurs before the facility is operational, because the announcement itself is what Moscow needs to deter and it will pick a moment of maximum political leverage rather than physical damage; the UAE technical trilateral either fails to convene or convenes without substantive Russian participation, and the proposed “peace framework” Trump wanted to publish before year-end is replaced by an interim ceasefire proposal Moscow will reject; European producers (MBDA, Diehl, Rheinmetall) publish their own memoranda of interest for co-production inside the same window, because a US licence sets a template European capitals cannot afford to be locked out of. What would collide the reading: a formal Congressional block on the export licence transfer (unlikely on a bipartisan reading of the Foreign Military Sales committee, but not impossible if the Republican appropriations chair insists on conditionality); a Trump administration reversal within thirty days on domestic political pressure (a Steve Bannon or Tucker Carlson-authored register on “forever wars” that Trump has proved responsive to); a direct Russian strike on any Raytheon-adjacent Ukrainian facility that forces Washington to choose between escalation and withdrawal of the licence; a Ukrainian domestic scandal at Ukroboronprom that voids the US counterparty credibility; a sudden Kremlin acceptance of the UAE track on terms that make the licence redundant. The base case is that the licence stands, the ground breaks, and the insertion holds — not because it is prudent, but because the industrial commitment, once signed, is easier to defend than to reverse.

Reading Note

The lenses used.

What’s driving this. Two layers carry the work. Structural: the specific mechanism is that a producer’s licence embeds Ukraine inside the American missile-defence industrial base at a depth from which the licence-giver, not the recipient, bears the higher reversal cost. Once Raytheon technicians are on Ukrainian soil and PAC-3 seekers are being machined under US export-controlled terms, unwinding the arrangement means either abandoning American plant to Russian discretion or writing off decades of prior export-control jurisprudence. That is not the sort of thing US administrations do casually. The commitment is asymmetric in duration: aid can be turned off next quarter; a co-production line cannot. Tactical: the specific mechanism is that Trump has taken exactly the escalatory step he spent nine months resisting and packaged it as an ally’s request granted — a surrender-tactic move that lets him claim the concession without owning the escalation, while extracting from Kyiv a stake-based dependency more binding than any aid promise Biden ever made.

What’s not driving this. Not the near-term battlefield. A production licence signed on 25 September delivers no additional interceptors before mid-2028 at the earliest, given site selection, hardening, technology transfer approvals under ITAR, and the eighteen-to-twenty-four-month typical spool-up on a new Patriot line. Anyone reading this as a fix for the current PAC-3 shortage has misread it. The current shortage is fixed by pulling from European or Israeli inventory or by accelerating existing Raytheon output at Lufkin and Andover, not by a Ukrainian plant that does not yet exist. The licence answers a different question — the durable-embedment question — and answering it does nothing for the next twelve months of Russian ballistic strikes.

What would break this reading.

  • A formal Congressional block on the ITAR licence transfer inside sixty days — a Republican Senate appropriations chair (Cotton or Wicker) attaching conditionality to the FY2027 NDAA that voids the Executive-level licence Trump has issued.
  • A Trump reversal within thirty days under domestic pressure — Steve Bannon, Tucker Carlson or the House Freedom Caucus authoring a “forever wars” register that Trump has proved responsive to when the political cost is legible.
  • A direct Russian conventional strike on any Raytheon-adjacent Ukrainian facility inside ninety days — a Kremlin escalation that forces Washington to choose between escalation of its own or a public withdrawal of the licence that voids the tactical logic.
  • An Ukroboronprom-scale corruption scandal that voids US counterparty credibility — the Ukrainian domestic register the licence is priced against remaining clean is a real constraint, and one that Zelenskyy’s own reforms have not fully secured.

Sources used. Sun Tzu’s Book III (subdue without fighting) reads the licence as position-substitution rather than escalation: the industrial fact does the work the troop commitment would have. Greene’s Twenty-Second Law (surrender as lever) names the concession as the instrument that converts an accumulating diplomatic tab into a stake-based obligation Kyiv now carries. Mearsheimer’s structural realism on small-state security by insertion into guarantor industrial architecture (TSMC, Yongsan-KORUS, and now Patriot-under-licence) reads the move as the same class of durable embedment those cases exemplify.

The Cambridge Compute

Akamai Technologies and Anthropic announced an $11.6-billion, multi-year cloud-services agreement on Thursday 25 September under which Akamai’s distributed network of 4,300-plus edge points of presence becomes the dedicated inference and edge-compute backbone for Anthropic’s Claude family. The stock jumped roughly 15 per cent in after-hours trading and closed Friday up about 12 per cent, adding more than $2 billion of market cap to a company that had traded for the past decade as a mature content-delivery utility. The same tape carried Microsoft up four per cent on the Copilot agentic-tools overhaul, and reports that OpenAI is in talks for a pre-IPO round at $1.2–1.5 trillion — a valuation larger than every listed European bank combined and roughly a quarter of Nvidia. This is what a real capital cycle looks like at the point where it stops being about frontier labs and starts being about who owns the pipes. The frontier lab is a customer now; the balance-sheet winners of 2027 will be the ones who can sell it inference at scale, and the market re-rated Akamai overnight because it woke up and remembered that fact.

Dalio on the infrastructure phase of a real capital cycle.

Ray Dalio’s reading of the long capital cycle, refined in his study of technology bubbles from the railway mania onward, is that every genuine wave produces two profit pools sequentially. The first pool goes to the visible protagonists — the labs, the founders, the frontier products — and it is smaller than it looks because their capital needs are enormous and their pricing power collapses as competitors arrive. The second, larger pool goes to whoever supplies the physical substrate the protagonists cannot build themselves: the rails, the transatlantic cable, the interstate fibre. The Cambridge Compute is the market discovering, in one after-hours session, that Akamai is a candidate for that second pool. Anthropic’s $11.6-billion commitment is not a favour; it is the acknowledgement that a model company at its stage cannot itself build 4,300 points of presence on six continents, that latency-to-user is now a competitive variable between Claude and its rivals, and that renting the pipes is cheaper than laying them. Dalio’s tell over the next four quarters is whether Cloudflare, Fastly, and Equinix trade at Akamai-like re-rating multiples on their own inference deals, or whether Akamai’s move is an idiosyncratic exception. The base case, given the visible pipeline of pending AI-infrastructure contracts, is broad re-rating: the second pool is beginning to price.

Greene’s Twenty-Fifth Law on the incumbent that survives by choosing a new category.

Re-create Yourself — Do Not Accept the Roles Society Foists on You. Greene’s discipline for the operator whose original category is dying is to refuse the identity the market has settled on you and to move, deliberately, into a category with a longer runway. Akamai was founded in 1998 to solve the problem of serving cached web content near the user, and by 2020 the CDN category it invented had commoditised: AWS CloudFront, Cloudflare’s free tier, and every hyperscaler’s own edge network had turned Akamai’s core business into a slow-growth utility. What CEO Tom Leighton has done, quietly, over the past three years is turn every one of those 4,300 edge nodes into a general-purpose compute location. The Cambridge Compute is the market seeing the re-creation for the first time and pricing it: Akamai is no longer a CDN whose margins compress; it is a distributed inference network whose margins expand as AI workloads shift closer to the user for latency and privacy reasons neither centralised cloud nor a frontier lab’s own datacentres can match. The Greene lesson generalises: incumbents die not because their category dies but because they refuse to name and enter the next one.

Munger on the incentive-caused reallocation of every marginal engineering dollar.

Charlie Munger’s formulation — “show me the incentive and I will show you the outcome” — reads the after-hours 15-per-cent print as the market’s incentive-caused reallocation instruction to every CTO, CFO, and board in the CDN and infrastructure category. When the market rewards you overnight with $2 billion of market cap for signing one AI-inference deal, every competitor in your sector receives a legible instruction: prioritise the AI-infrastructure sales motion above every other line item. Cloudflare’s workers-AI product, Fastly’s Compute@Edge, Equinix’s bare-metal offering — each will be re-priced by their own boards in the coming weeks against the new Akamai multiple, and each will accelerate the capital commitments the AI-inference layer requires. Munger’s tell over ninety days is that at least two competitors publish comparable framework agreements with OpenAI, Meta, xAI or Mistral, at deal sizes above $3 billion. If they do, the Cambridge Compute is a regime change. If they do not, it is a signature deal Anthropic paid a premium for.

What the reading expects.

Base case through end-Q1 2027: Akamai holds most of the two-day re-rating, closing 2026 at least ten per cent above its pre-announcement level, absent a broader tech-sector drawdown; at least one comparable AI-infrastructure agreement above $3 billion prints from Cloudflare, Fastly, or Equinix within ninety days, extending the re-rating across the peer group; OpenAI closes a pre-IPO round at a headline valuation between $1.1 trillion and $1.4 trillion before end-2026, cementing the shape of the capital cycle even as the IPO itself slips into 2027; Anthropic’s next primary round prices at above $250 billion, roughly triple the mid-2026 mark, on the strength of a demonstrated ability to sign multi-year infrastructure at scale; hyperscalers respond by publicly matching or exceeding the Akamai-Anthropic arrangement, either through internal edge investment or acquisition of a smaller distributed compute player (Fastly is the natural target); AI bubble concerns remain elevated in commentary but do not produce a settlement-eligible burst before end-2026 because the capital commitments now on the books create a floor under revenue for the infrastructure layer even if frontier-lab economics stress. What would collide the reading: a public re-cut of the Akamai-Anthropic terms within sixty days that reveals the announced number was pipeline rather than contracted revenue (an accounting tell that would compress the multiple back to CDN levels); an OpenAI or Anthropic capital-raise that fails to clear at the guided range (a demand-side tell that the second-pool thesis is priced ahead of the fundamentals); a hyperscaler announcement of an edge-inference build-out at a scale that makes the third-party distributed model uneconomic; a US export-control move against advanced inference hardware that fragments the demand curve; a broader equity-market drawdown above 12 per cent that pulls the whole cohort lower regardless of narrative. The base case is that the infrastructure re-rating widens, the OpenAI round closes, and the AI capital cycle enters its second, more durable phase.

The Bercy Impasse

Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu will present the 2027 draft budget in the coming days, aiming for a 5.0-per-cent-of-GDP deficit against the 5.4 per cent the 2026 baseline slipped to when growth undershot and interest costs overshot. The number is calibrated against two constraints that no longer reconcile: the National Rally and La France Insoumise together control enough seats to move a motion of censure against anything they judge austere, and the bond market is now pricing the OAT-Bund spread as though austerity is the only path off the fiscal escalator. On Friday the 10-year OAT closed above 4.5 per cent for the first time since 2008 and the spread over Bunds cleared 90 basis points. Two prime ministers have fallen on budgets since December 2024: Michel Barnier on the 2025 finance bill, François Bayrou on the 2026 one. Lecornu, in office since February 2026 after using Article 49.3 to force through the 2026 budget without a vote, is now the third premier to write the same problem down. France’s political system has spent three years demonstrating that no configuration of the National Assembly can vote to reduce the deficit; the bond market has spent this week pricing what happens when that becomes common knowledge.

Aristotle on the mixed constitution that produces stalemate rather than moderation.

Aristotle’s Politics Book IV analyses the mixed constitution — the arrangement that blends elements of oligarchy and democracy on the theory that opposed interests will discipline each other into moderation. His warning, generally forgotten by admirers of the idea, is that the mix works only when the constituent factions each control enough power to block outcomes they cannot tolerate but not enough to impose their own. When the balance tips too far in either direction, the constitution collapses into stasis: the parties block each other from acting, but no single party can hold the state together on its own. The 2024-elected Assemblée Nationale is Aristotle’s warning made concrete. Neither the RN-and-allied right, the LFI-and-allied left, nor the centrist Ensemble bloc holds a working majority; any two of them together can block anything the third proposes, but no combination of two of them can agree on a joint programme. Every budget since is a document written to be less unacceptable to the median obstructive coalition than to be genuinely acceptable to any positive one — and the OAT market has now noticed that the arithmetic of that game admits no path to a primary surplus in the current parliament, however patient the writer.

Dalio on the fiscal end-phase that arrives as a political crisis.

Ray Dalio’s Big Cycle framing of late-stage sovereign debt cycles observes that the fiscal breaking point is almost never an arithmetic one — the debt-to-GDP ratio at which the crisis arrives is not the debt-to-GDP ratio at which the crisis was mathematically inevitable. Japan sustains 260 per cent debt-to-GDP indefinitely; Argentina defaults at 60 per cent. What decides the difference is political: whether the sovereign’s political system can author, when necessary, either a fiscal consolidation (spending cuts, tax rises, entitlement reform) or an inflation-and-repression arrangement (financial repression, negative real rates, capital controls) that transfers the adjustment cost from bondholders to somebody else. France’s political system, in Dalio’s reading, has demonstrated over three consecutive budget cycles that it can author neither. The RN will not vote for spending cuts; the LFI will not vote for entitlement reform; the centre cannot lead an austerity coalition without the wings; the ECB’s Transmission Protection Instrument is available to buy OATs only if France is in an EDP-compliant programme, which requires the political consolidation that cannot happen. Dalio’s end-phase tell is not the OAT spread; it is the OAT auction whose bid-to-cover falls below 1.8 despite the yield offered, at which point the ECB is forced to price its willingness to run TPI without an EDP framework, which forces Berlin to price its willingness to accept eurozone shared liability without the discipline the Bundesbank has spent three decades demanding.

La Rochefoucauld on the coalition that lasts until the more remunerative betrayal appears.

François de La Rochefoucauld’s Maxims observe repeatedly that political coalitions are held together not by shared belief but by the calculation that the coalition delivers more than defection would. The moment a member finds a more remunerative arrangement outside the coalition, the coalition ends. Marine Le Pen’s Rassemblement National spent 2024-2026 tacitly protecting first Barnier, then Bayrou, and now Lecornu from votes of censure it could have won, because she calculated that a presidential election under a discredited incumbent government suited her 2027 arithmetic better than the immediate collapse of the Fifth Republic. That calculation is now marginally reversing: with OATs above 4.5 per cent, the CAC 40 selling off in sympathy, and the household mortgage rate reset arriving in 2027, the RN’s domestic economic argument is now easier to make against a still-incumbent Lecornu than against a fresh premier the RN would face in an early election it did not want. La Rochefoucauld’s tell over ninety days is whether Le Pen’s public posture toward the 2027 budget shifts from “we will vote it down if it contains austerity” to “we will vote down any budget by this government,” because the second formulation is the one that ends the coalition of convenience holding the Fifth Republic’s current political configuration in place.

What the reading expects.

Base case through end-Q1 2027: Lecornu presents a 2027 draft budget targeting the 5.0-per-cent deficit but containing enough tactical concessions (a symbolic tax on the highest incomes, a slower phase-in of the pension reform, a defence-spending increase to shield the ministry from cuts) to make an RN or PS abstention on censure marginally plausible; a formal motion of censure is filed by LFI within the first parliamentary reading window; the motion falls short of the 289-vote threshold by 15-40 votes, because the PS abstains and the RN abstains on the first attempt to preserve the option value of the second attempt; Fitch downgrades France to AA- by end-Q4 2026, Moody’s follows to Aa3 by end-Q1 2027; the OAT-Bund spread trades a 90-130 basis-point band with occasional excursions above; ECB TPI activation is discussed at the Governing Council but not deployed formally, because deployment without an EDP framework opens a north-south political fight the ECB cannot win in Q4; Lecornu passes the 2027 budget via Article 49.3 by late Q1 2027, surviving the resulting censure vote by a narrow margin; a Macron-authored dissolution of the Assembly is discussed publicly but not called before end-Q1 2027, because polling still favours the RN in an early election. What would collide the reading: an RN decision to file censure themselves rather than allow LFI to lead (a domestic political tell that Le Pen has moved into full opposition posture); a failed OAT auction with bid-to-cover below 1.8 (a bond-market tell that forces an emergency ECB decision within 48 hours); a Macron dissolution of the Assembly in Q4 (a presidential tell that the constitutional arithmetic is now unrecoverable through parliamentary means); an ECB TPI activation without an EDP framework (a Frankfurt tell that the eurozone has crossed into fiscal-monetary co-ordination without political-union backing); a Bundestag or CDU parliamentary-group formal position against TPI use for France (a Berlin tell that closes the mutualisation door). Any of the five would move the reading from “Lecornu survives on 49.3, spread trades wide but contained” toward either “government falls and early elections deliver an RN-led National Assembly by mid-2027” or “Frankfurt breaks the eurozone rules to save France, and the political fallout in Berlin reshapes the Merz coalition.”

Reading Note

The lenses used.

What’s driving this. Two layers carry the work. Structural: the specific mechanism is that France’s combined debt-to-GDP (now above 115 per cent), 5.4-per-cent structural deficit, one-trillion-euro annual gross issuance calendar, and non-zero rollover premium have arrived at the point where the debt-service line is growing faster than any plausible primary-balance improvement the parliamentary arithmetic can vote for. This is the Dalio end-phase fiscal condition, and its onset was arithmetically clear years ago; what shifted this week is that the OAT market began pricing it into the current benchmark rather than the tail. Institutional: the specific mechanism is that the 2024-elected Assemblée Nationale contains three roughly-equal blocs (right, left, centre) none of which can govern and any two of which can block, in a constitutional structure (Fifth Republic) whose primary conflict-resolution instrument (presidential dissolution) has now been used and produced this exact deadlock. Aristotle’s mixed-constitution warning — the balance that produces stalemate rather than moderation — describes the current configuration precisely.

What’s not driving this. Not the eurozone as a whole. Italian BTP-Bund spreads have widened only marginally in sympathy; Spanish and Portuguese spreads are essentially unchanged. This is not a euro crisis; it is a France crisis inside the euro, and the OAT move reflects an idiosyncratic political-fiscal blockage rather than a systemic revaluation of eurozone sovereign risk. A reader who codes this as 2011-2012 redux has mistaken the file. What is happening is closer to a slow-motion Greek 2010 with a hundred times the balance sheet: an individual sovereign whose domestic politics has proved incapable of the adjustment its arithmetic requires, and whose next-step response set (TPI, dissolution, coalition realignment) is politically expensive at every option.

What would break this reading.

  • A Macron dissolution of the National Assembly inside ninety days — a presidential tell that Macron has judged the current parliament unrecoverable and prefers to precipitate the fight on his own timing rather than the RN’s. Polling still favours the RN in an early election, so this remains the low-probability branch.
  • A failed OAT auction with bid-to-cover below 1.8 despite the yield offered — a bond-market tell that forces the ECB and the German government to price TPI-versus-Bundesbank inside 48 hours, and that would compress the OAT-Bund spread through central-bank intervention rather than through fiscal consolidation.
  • An RN decision to file censure directly, rather than allow LFI to lead the motion — a Le Pen tell that the calculation has shifted from “preserve the discredited incumbent” to “precipitate the collapse under the incumbent’s name”, and that ends the tacit coalition of convenience.
  • A Bundestag or CDU parliamentary-group formal position against TPI use for France, published before Q1 2027 — a Berlin tell that closes the mutualisation door and forces the ECB back onto instruments it cannot deploy without political cover.

Sources used. Aristotle’s Politics IV on the mixed-constitution stalemate names the arithmetic condition of the current Assembly with unusual precision. Dalio’s Big Cycle on the late-stage sovereign debt cycle whose breaking point is political rather than arithmetic reads France’s inability to consolidate as the diagnostic that decides the OAT market’s pricing. La Rochefoucauld on the interest-based coalition that holds only until the more remunerative betrayal appears names the RN’s calculation as the load-bearing variable for whether the current premier lasts a month or the year.

Use the surrender tactic — transform weakness into power. Trump grants Ukraine a Patriot production licence, and the concession installs an American industrial base inside Ukrainian sovereignty from which Washington bears the higher reversal cost. Each story rewards the reader who names who is actually paying the reversal cost, and who has installed themselves inside the counterparty in a form the counterparty will find harder to remove than the counterparty realises. Patriot Licence: Trump appears to concede; Kyiv appears to gain; the reversal cost sits with Washington, because dismantling an American production line on Ukrainian soil is harder than turning off aid ever was. Cambridge Compute: Anthropic appears to be the customer; Akamai appears to be the supplier; the market has just priced who owns the more durable position — the party with 4,300 physical edge nodes, not the party with a model. Bercy Impasse: Lecornu appears to be the operator writing the budget; the RN appears to be the opposition threatening censure; the OAT market has just priced who actually holds the constitutional whip — neither of them, because the Assembly arithmetic itself does.

In your own affairs: the discipline cuts three ways. On the concession you granted last quarter, ask whose reversal cost is now higher — yours or the recipient’s — and price the answer honestly. On the vendor you signed with last month, ask whether you rented capacity or installed a supplier inside your product with a switching cost you have not yet inventoried. And on the committee you sit on whose votes never seem to produce a decision, ask whether the block-any-three-can-form is the accidental output of the individual voters or the deliberate structural feature the person who designed the committee wanted.
